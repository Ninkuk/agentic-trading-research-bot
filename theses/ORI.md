## 1. Verdict and thesis

**BUY at $41.79 (live quote).** kill-thesis: **SOUND (on this pass)** —
conditions=5 (4 probable, 1 plausible), refuted=0, unknown=2.

Old Republic is a conservatively run specialty P&C and title insurer — 103
years old, 46 consecutive years of regular-dividend raises — earning a 12.1%
annualized operating ROE against a ~8.2% cost of equity, with both segments
managed to a stated 90–95% combined-ratio band and essentially all operating
earnings returned (regular dividend + year-end specials + buybacks). At
$41.79 the price implies an 8.4–10.5% annual return on honest ex-gains
operating earnings — at or above the hurdle on every scenario including a
floor that charges growth its full capital cost. The headline cheapness (P/E
9.2, 16.4% FCF yield) is a mark-to-market illusion; the real bargain is
smaller but still positive, on a business whose downside is cushioned by a
$17B investment portfolio and a full-payout regime.

**Closest attack:** the reserve-cycle attack. Q4 2025 raised the 2025
accident-year pick; Q2 2026 specialty development flipped net unfavorable
(+0.3 pts) on a $40M strengthening in the runoff transactional-risk book;
workers' comp favorable development — the industry's great reservoir — came

long-tail lines, the conservative-reserving premise (condition 1) is gone and
the floor case with it.

## 2. Business

**Created:** Old Republic sells promises that hold. Specialty Insurance
(~64% of premiums) underwrites long-tail commercial coverages — trucking/
commercial auto, workers' comp, general liability, E&O/D&O, auto warranty —
for businesses that need a counterparty who will still be solvent when a
claim settles a decade later; the customer buys certainty, tailored
underwriting, and claims-handling in niches large generalists serve badly.
Title Insurance (~36%) sells the one-time assurance that a property's title
is clean — a product bought at closing, priced off transaction values, with
tiny loss ratios (3.0% in Q2) and huge expense ratios (92.1%).

**Captured:** three distinct mechanisms. (1) Underwriting margin: premiums
minus losses and expenses — both segments target 90–95% combined ratios over
a cycle, i.e. a 5–10% pre-investment margin on premiums. (2) Float income:
$15.3B of loss reserves plus $4.6B unearned premiums fund a ~$17.5B
investment portfolio (84% bonds/short-term at a 4.8% book yield, 16%
equities) whose income — $182M in Q2, ~40% of pretax operating income — is
capital the policyholders lent for free. (3) Title agency economics: 78% of
title revenue flows through independent agents, ORI keeping an underwriting
sliver of each premium at near-zero balance-sheet cost.

**Protected:** no single spectacular moat; several honest mechanisms.
Regulated scale (licenses, statutory capital, rate filings in every state)
plus a century of loss data in niche long-tail lines where pricing without
history is how new entrants die; distribution relationships (independent
title agents; specialty program partners like the auto-warranty channel);
and the title business is a four-carrier oligopoly (ORI is one of the "big
four", per the 10-K). The protection is adequacy, not dominance: management's
own Q2 words are that competition is intensifying and property rates fell
7.5%. Where the moat fails, the balance sheet — not the franchise — is the
backstop.

**Operating leverage (Phase 0): positive on the reported line, flat
ex-investment-gains.** FY revenue → operating income ($M, stockanalysis
income-statement route; `gainInvestmentRevIns` in parentheses):

investment income both compound while margins sit below the stated band.
