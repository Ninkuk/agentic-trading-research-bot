## 2. Business

**Created:** a guest gets a place to stay that a hotel cannot supply — a whole
home, in a residential neighbourhood, usually cheaper per person for a group
and per night for a long stay, in cities where hotel inventory is scarce or
expensive. What is actually being sold is *trust in a stranger's house*: the
reviews, the identity verification, the payment held in escrow until check-in,
the 24-hour support, and the guarantee that someone stands behind the booking.
That trust layer is the whole product; without it the listings are Craigslist.
A host gets demand and a payments/tax/insurance stack they could not assemble
alone — Airbnb collects and remits lodging taxes in ~33,000 jurisdictions on
hosts' behalf, which is a compliance burden no individual host would carry.
The surplus is visible in behaviour, not assertion: 64% of nights are booked
in Airbnb's own app rather than through a search engine, and first-time-booker
growth accelerated to 11% in Q2 2026.

**Captured:** not "a commission" — five distinct mechanisms.
(i) The core split fee on homes: guests pay a service fee and hosts pay a host
fee, together producing an implied take rate of about 13.2% of gross booking
value ($3,608M revenue on $27.2B of Q2 GBV = 13.26%). (ii) A simplified
single service fee now being migrated across the supply base — management
expects the entire supply base on it by year-end 2026 — which shifts the fee
onto the host side and lets guests see one price. (iii) Hotels: boutique and
independent hotel inventory in 20+ destinations, a "single-digit percentage of
nights booked" but growing roughly three times as fast as homes, and carrying
what management calls "an extremely favorable take rate" versus other
platforms. (iv) Services and Experiences: grocery delivery, car rental, airport
pickup, luggage storage, resort passes, plus Experiences supply up ~80% YoY in
Q2 — small today, and currently a *drag* on take rate because they are being
seeded with customer incentives. (v) The balance sheet itself: $680M of
trailing interest income, a fifth of pretax income, earned on $12.07B of
corporate cash and investments plus the float held on hosts' behalf between
booking and check-in. Travel insurance is a sixth, small and high-margin.
A seventh is announced but not launched — Chesky (Goldman Communacopia,
2026-09-08) called host-side sponsored listings "a pretty easy straight shot to
$1 billion incremental high margin revenue." That is *possible* tier: no
product, no date, no revenue. It belongs in §6, not in the condition list.

**Protected:** the mechanism is a two-sided liquidity loop with a switching
cost that sits on the *guest* side, which is unusual. Supply is the moat's
raw material — millions of unique, non-fungible homes that no competitor can
replicate by writing a cheque, because each one is an individual's asset with
its own reviews and its own accumulated trust score. A hotel room at Booking
and at Expedia is the same room; a home on Airbnb exists nowhere else. That
gives Airbnb something Booking and Expedia structurally lack: inventory
exclusivity. The second mechanism is that the brand has become the category
verb, which converts into the 64% direct-app share and means Airbnb is not
renting its demand from Google — the single most important defence against
both search-cost inflation and AI-agent intermediation.

The honest limits. The moat protects the *homes* business, not the new
verticals: hotels, car rental and grocery are commodity inventory where Airbnb
is the marginal, sub-scale entrant against incumbents with better supply
relationships. And the moat is one-sided on price — a host who accumulates
direct-booking demand can leave, which is exactly what the reported 6%/10%
fee pilot is defending against. A moat that requires a 40% price cut to hold
its most professional supply is a moat under load.

**Control:** four share classes. Class A one vote (423.6M outstanding at
2026-01-31), Class B twenty votes (175.9M), Class C non-voting (zero
outstanding, 2.0B authorised), Class H non-voting (9.2M, the host endowment).
Class B therefore carries ~3,519M of ~3,942M votes — about 89% of voting power
— and the founders hold the bulk of it. This forecloses an unsolicited
takeover and any activist path entirely; the 2.0B authorised Class C shares
mean the company can also issue equity without diluting founder control at
all. There is no external mechanism to change strategy or capital allocation
at Airbnb. That is a governance fact, not a complaint — but it means the
"management is unlikely to destroy the cash flow" leg of any thesis here rests
on trusting three people, with no recourse.

## 3. Threads pulled

**The FX tailwind inside the "acceleration" (load-bearing, and it holds up
only partly).** The headline story is that growth re-accelerated from 10% in
2025 to 17% in Q2 2026. The shareholder letter discloses the ex-FX figures the
call did not lead with: revenue +13% ex-FX, GBV +15% ex-FX against +16%
reported. The Q3 guide of 15–17% is explicitly stated to include "an
approximate three percentage point FX tailwind after factoring in our hedging
program." So the real acceleration is 10% → ~13%, not 10% → 17%, and about a
third of the reported improvement is currency that laps out. Nights and Seats
Booked — the volume metric currency cannot touch — grew 10%. This is why
condition 1 is *plausible* and not *probable*: three points of the headline
are borrowed.

**The other thing inside the acceleration: a home-market World Cup.** The
FIFA World Cup 2026 ran 11 June – 19 July 2026 across the US, Canada and
Mexico — North America is 42% of revenue by listing location. Airbnb was an
official Tournament Supporter; the shareholder letter says it "hosted millions
of guest arrivals during the tournament" and that "more than 150,000 homes
across host cities were listed on Airbnb for the first time." Three weeks of
the tournament fall in Q2 2026 and the larger part in Q3 2026 — precisely the
two quarters carrying both the acceleration and the ~3pp FX tailwind. The
company does not size the contribution and almost certainly never will
(§6.7). Management frames major events as "a repeatable playbook that scales
across global, regional, and local events," and the Olympics/Tour de
France/La Liga/NASCAR partnerships are real — but a World Cup in your largest
market is not repeatable inside the horizon: the next one is 2030, in Spain,
Portugal and Morocco. This is the second borrowed component of a headline
that reads as structural re-acceleration, and it is why condition 1's decisive
test is the FY2027 guide rather than any 2026 print.

**The take-rate pilot (the most important post-call event).** Bloomberg,
reported 2026-08-31 via MT Newswires: Airbnb is testing cutting its take rate
to 6% or 10% from an average of ~16% for some US hosts, limited to hosts using
third-party listing-management software, and requiring guests to book through
a unique host-supplied link. An Airbnb spokesperson confirmed the test. The
stated purpose — "retain bookings that hosts might otherwise direct to their
own websites" — names the mechanism: professional hosts with their own demand
are the segment most able to disintermediate, and the defence is price. This
is consistent with, and sharper than, what management said on the Q2 call
about the single service fee having "a kind of downward pressure on pricing."
It is not in any filing; it is a press report with a company confirmation, so
it is tiered as low-confidence colour with a primary-adjacent confirmation.
It is the reason condition 3 is a condition at all.

alone: ABNB −4.1%, BKNG −6.7%, EXPE −7.9%, MAR −2.3%, SPY −0.5%. The

**Integrity checks.**

- *Reinvestment / terminal-ROE warning, answered.* Scenarios A, B, D, E and G
  trip the tool's "growth without reinvestment" warning, because reported FCF
  exceeds trailing net income, leaving a negative implied reinvestment rate.
  The correct answer here is not to cut terminal growth — it is already at the
  10-year breakeven, i.e. pure nominal inflation with no real growth, which
  needs no reinvestment. The deeper point is definitional: Airbnb's
  reinvestment is *expensed*, not capitalised. It spends $2.5B/yr on product
  development and $4.9B/yr on sales and marketing, all of it already deducted
  before FCF; capex is $33M. A sales-to-capital reinvestment model is not
  applicable to this business, and the negative reinvestment rate is an
  artefact of that, not evidence of a free lunch. Scenarios C and F, which
  normalise the cash-tax deferral, produce a reinvestment rate of ~0 and
  therefore implied terminal ROEs of 302% and 6,351% — the same artefact,
  read from the other side, and further reason to treat the terminal ROE
  diagnostic as inapplicable rather than as a green light.
- *Base-year cash tax vs marginal.* Fails the check and is handled: 6.6% cash
  versus a 20.0% book rate and a 21% US federal statutory rate. Scenarios C
  and F carry the $450M haircut; the $2,140M net DTA gives roughly five years
  before the shield is gone.
- *Market-share sentence.* At 11% compounded for five years, revenue goes
  from $13.16B to ~$22.2B, which at the current 13.2% implied take rate is
  ~$168B of gross booking value against roughly $99B trailing. That is
  Booking-Holdings-scale intermediation for a company that today books
  148.3M nights and seats a quarter. It does not require most of the
  accommodation market — but the denominator is not sourced in this run
  (the 10-K gives no TAM estimate), so the fraction is left unstated rather
  than invented. See §6.
- *Terminal growth vs the Item 1A terminal risk.* The dominant structural risk
  in the 10-K risk factors is city- and country-level restriction of
  short-term rental supply — the disclosure names New York City's 2023
  regulations as "a de facto ban" and warns that other cities have passed
  onerous restrictions — compounded by an unusually aggressive tax posture
  toward the platform: three Italian settlements totalling €894M ($957M) for
