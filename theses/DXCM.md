## 2. Business

**Created:** a person with diabetes otherwise learns their glucose by
pricking a finger four to eight times a day and gets a handful of dots. A
DexCom sensor, worn on the body for 10 or 15 days, streams a reading every
five minutes to a phone, with alarms for lows. The value the customer
receives is not the number — it is the removal of an unpredictable,
frightening failure mode, plus the ability to see what a meal or a walk
actually did. The CONNECT randomized trial (ADA 2026) put a size on it for
type-2 patients who take no insulin at all: 1.6% A1C improvement, 0.9% better
than control, five extra hours per day in range, sustained over 26 weeks, and
97% median device utilization — patients who are handed this do not put it
down. A CVS Health real-world study over three years found a 66% reduction in
diabetes-related hospitalizations. The surplus over the price is large, which
is why utilization runs above 80% in DexCom's own registry wherever coverage
exists.

**Captured:** three distinct mechanisms, not one.
*(a) The consumable.* The sensor is the razor blade. Revenue is a function of
patients × sensors per patient-year × net price per sensor. This is ~85% of
revenue and it recurs by biology, not by renewal decision.
*(b) The reimbursement franchise.* DexCom does not sell to patients; it sells
to payers. FY2025 revenue split 84.9% distributor / 15.1% direct
($3,959.0M / $703.0M), and in the US it is 96% distributor — the DME and
pharmacy channels. Every incremental dollar of revenue comes from winning a
coverage decision, not a shelf. That is why the whole company's forward story
is a list of payer events, and why the price is set by formulary negotiation
rather than by the patient's willingness to pay.
*(c) Cash-pay consumer.* Stelo, the over-the-counter sensor launched 2024,
did $100M in its first twelve months and by sell-side estimate has plateaued
around $35–42M/quarter. Management frames it explicitly as a feeder into
type-2 commercial coverage rather than as a business — "if a patient does not
have coverage, there is Stelo" (CFO, 2026-09-09).

**Protected:** the mechanism is regulatory and reimbursement accumulation,
not technology. Anyone can build a glucose sensor; DexCom has spent twenty
years assembling (i) FDA clearances at the *iCGM* level that permit
integration into automated insulin delivery, (ii) the actual integrations —
Tandem/Mobi, Insulet, Beta Bionics all connected to G7 15-day as of Q2'26 —
which make the sensor a component of a closed-loop system the patient cannot
casually swap out, (iii) reimbursement contracts with all four largest
commercial PBMs, and (iv) the clinical evidence base (DIaMonD, MOBILE, now
CONNECT) that shifts standards of care and therefore what CMS is willing to
pay for. Each of those is slow to build and each raises the cost of a new
entrant. What it does **not** protect against is the one competitor who has
already paid the same entry cost: Abbott, whose Libre platform competes
directly, whose own CMS type-2 petition is pending in parallel (on a
narrower "any glucose-lowering medication" basis), and which received FDA
authorization for Libre Duo, the first dual glucose-ketone sensor, in the
same window. Nor does it protect against the payer itself: the moat's
foundation is that CMS pays a certain amount, and CMS has already announced
it intends to pay less from 2028.

**Control:** one class of common stock, one vote per share, no controlling
holder — insiders own 0.243% and institutions 104.8% (`stocks.db` `v_latest`;
the >100% figure is the usual double-count from securities lending). The
charter and bylaws carry blank-check preferred issuable without stockholder
approval, no cumulative voting, no action by written consent, a restricted
right to call special meetings, advance-notice nomination, a federal forum
provision, and DGCL §203. That combination forecloses a hostile tender and
makes an activist path slow, but there is no family, founder or strategic
block — the null answer on ownership, with a standard Delaware fence.

## 3. Threads pulled

**The 15-day sensor is a cost story that the revenue line has to survive.**
Extending wear from 10 to 15 days cuts sensors per patient-year from ~36.5 to
~24.3 — a third fewer units. Management talks about it exclusively as gross
margin ("15-Day... really starts to anchor up the gross margin," CFO
2026-09-09) and never as a revenue headwind, which implies the product is
priced at parity per patient-month rather than per sensor. Q2'26 corroborates
it: gross margin +400bp YoY to 64.1% while revenue still grew 13%. The
economics therefore run the right way — same revenue per patient, a third
less COGS — and the tailwind is not yet mostly in the numbers: the US base
enters 2027 at ~50% converted versus 0% entering 2026, and international
conversion has only just begun (Health Canada is the first non-US regulator
to clear it). This is the single strongest fact in the bull case and it is
priced.

**The base free cash flow is flattered by a tax holiday with a date on it.**
FY2025 income taxes *paid* were $94.4M (Federal $68.0M, State $10.6M, Foreign
$15.8M) against a book income tax expense of $252.1M — an 8.7% cash rate
against a 23.2% book rate, with $182.2M of deferred tax expense as the
plug. The 10-K names the cause: "the One Big Beautiful Bill Act... The
primary impact of this legislation is a reduction in our U.S. income tax
liability and deferred tax asset related to the ability to currently deduct
U.S.-based research expenses against U.S. income." This is the §174 catch-up
on previously capitalized R&D (~$600M/yr of R&D), and it is transitory by
construction — once the accumulated balance is deducted, cash taxes converge
to book. It is **not** an NOL story: federal NOLs are down to $3.8M. The
remaining carryforwards are California-only ($162.0M NOL expiring 2037,
$134.6M R&D credits, indefinite) and cannot carry a 14-point federal-plus-
state gap. §4 haircuts the base by the $157.7M FY2025 gap. This is the
finding that turns a marginal pass into a clear one.

**CMS gives with one hand and takes with the other, and only one half has a
number.** The bull half: petitions filed early 2025 by DexCom (broad, "a
diagnosis with type 2") and by Abbott (narrower, "any glucose-lowering
medication"), both pending; CONNECT read out at ADA 2026; the plan assumes
coverage ~mid-2027 and the CFO reaffirmed a proposal "by year-end"
2026-09-09. The bear half sits in Item 1A: CMS has reclassified CGM and
insulin pumps as items requiring "frequent and substantial servicing," will
phase in monthly rental payments, will bundle the receiver's amortized cost
(over a 5-year useful life) into the supply fee, will price at a percentile
of winning bids rather than the maximum, will recompete at least every three
years, and will extend competitive-bidding prices to non-competitive-bidding
areas — with a new "country of origin" question aimed at exactly DexCom's
Malaysia and Ireland manufacturing. The company's own words: "we expect that
Medicare reimbursement for our CGM systems will decrease beginning in 2028."
The magnitude is the single largest unquantified input to the terminal value
(§6.2). The same risk factor also carries the OBBBA Medicaid provisions —
CBO estimates 11.8 million more uninsured by 2034.

**Competitive position: taking share, from a competitor that is also getting
better.** The CFO's claim (2026-09-09) is that DexCom has been taking script
share from Abbott, attributed to coverage rather than product — "when you
look at where our competitor has gone, they've gone down the cash pay route
... we've done really well when it came to coverage." He also volunteered
that DexCom's total patient base grew ~20% in 2025 and is guided to high
teens in 2026, while conceding "a competitor's revenue has changed over the
same period." Against that: Abbott has FDA clearance to integrate Libre into
AID systems and is pursuing third-party pump integrations, has the first
dual-analyte (glucose-ketone) sensor authorized, and has a parallel CMS
petition. DexCom's answer on ketones is that they are "a feature" addressing
mainly euglycemic DKA in SGLT2 users, and that the DKA unmet need is being
*off* a sensor rather than lacking a ketone read — a coherent argument, but
it is the incumbent explaining why the entrant's new feature does not matter,
which is a claim to discount rather than accept.
