## 1. Verdict and thesis

**PASS at $29.94 (last regular-session trade, 2026-09-11).** kill-thesis:
**FLAWED** — conditions=5 (4 probable, 1 plausible), refuted=3, unknown=0,

**p(beat SPY, 63 td): 0.42** · kill-thesis: 0.42 · **Disputed expectation:**
at $29.94 the market implies roughly 7.2%/yr on the reported cash flow — about
200bp below a 9.21% cost of equity and below even the US median cost of
capital of 7.79%. What revises it upward is the Q1 FY2027 print (2026-11-19)
showing US insurance units flat or positive once the lost account laps, with
gross margin recovering above 44%; what revises it downward is another quarter
of mid-single-digit unit declines with OpEx per car still rising.

Copart is still a very good business — 28.4% ROIC, $4.4B net cash, no debt, an
entrenched two-player salvage structure, and pricing power that raised revenue
per unit 5.7% in a year when units fell 5.5%. It is not a good price. FY2026
was the year the flywheel stopped: revenue +0.4%, operating income −2.6%,
operating margin down from 37.7% to 35.4% (39.3% in FY2022), US insurance units
−8%, gross margin in Q4 down 350bp to 41.8% while OpEx per car rose 12.7%.
Every reverse-DCF scenario tested — bear to bull, before and after ACV — lands
between 5.5% and 7.8% a year against a 9.21% hurdle; the price needs about 12%
FCF growth for five years, against 3.76% consensus revenue growth and negative
core units. Management then spent $1.9B of the cash pile on a company with
−$45.7M TTM operating income and $29.1M of FCF against $53.1M of stock
compensation, and told the Street the deal is EPS-neutral in year one. Nothing
here is broken; the arithmetic simply does not pay.

**Closest attack:** the pricing engine. Revenue per unit rose 5.4% in Q4 and
5.7% for the year; global ASPs rose 3.5%, outpacing the Manheim index at 2.8%;
total loss frequency hit a record 23.3% and collision severity is up 8.8%.
Those are real, durable, and the reason revenue grew at all. If the unit
decline is the lapped account plus a claim-frequency trough, the same pricing
on flat units puts fee revenue back to high single digits and the 12% path
becomes arguable. It survives the attack — it just was not enough: operating
income fell anyway.
