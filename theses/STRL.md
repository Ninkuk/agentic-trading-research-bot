## 2. Business

**Created:** Sterling turns raw land into a build-ready campus — mass
grading, drainage, utilities, thousands of miles of underground pipe and
conduit, foundations — and, through CEC, the electrical and mechanical
fit-out behind it, for customers whose binding constraint is time: a
hyperscaler or chip fab running months ahead of schedule is worth more to
the owner than the contract is worth to Sterling (the CEO's Micron example:
a package due in September delivered in July; GCs carry liquidated damages
of $250–400K a day; Laguna, 2026-09-15). The customer surplus is schedule.
Customers come to Sterling because it has the scale (4,400 employees, a
fleet it is upsizing with Caterpillar), the vertical integration (its own
aggregates, hauling, paving) and the 3D/LIDAR/drone modelling to deliver
300–10,000-acre sites faster than regional earthmovers can; it is being
pulled into new geographies by the same customers (Q2 call).

**Captured:** three distinct mechanisms. (i) E-Infrastructure site
development — phased, mostly negotiated follow-on work at 24.1% adjusted
operating margin (Q2), high-20s for the legacy site business; margins rise
through a campus because later phases are negotiated, not bid, and
vertical integration removes cost the bid still carried ("I bid that cost
in like everybody else does, I just know I'll never use it", Laguna). (ii)
CEC electrical/mechanical — ~12% adjusted margin, growing 140% y/y, where
value is captured by prefab (15–20% labour saving) and by exiting
loss-making legacy lines by end-2027. (iii) Transportation (19.5% adjusted
margin, 13% of revenue, state DOTs, bid work being wound down into
E-Infrastructure) and Building Solutions (residential concrete/plumbing in
DFW, Houston, Phoenix; 9.9%, in a housing downturn). Backlog margin was
17.8% at 2025 year-end (10-K).

**Protected:** a mechanism, but a cyclical one. What stops a competitor
tomorrow is capacity plus incumbency: there is "nobody that has the scale"
on the site side (management's claim, Laguna), the customer's own planning
horizon has stretched to five years and Sterling sits inside it, and once
on a campus it has never lost a follow-on phase (Q2 call — a management
claim, unverifiable from filings). The moat is strongest exactly when
demand exceeds industry capacity — which is now, and which is why the
company can "turn down a lot more projects than people realize". When
demand normalises, selectivity ends and site-development margins have no
structural reason to stay at 2× the industry's. The electrical side has a
weaker protection story: the binding constraint is journeyman electricians,
and the CEO's own account is that the big players win by paying $5–20/hour
more — a labour-cost war, not a moat.

**Control:** one share, one vote, no controller (10-K: one vote per share,
no cumulative voting, no preferred outstanding; insiders 2.13%,
institutions 86.8%). Blank-check preferred and Delaware anti-takeover
provisions are the only forecloses (10-K Item 1A). An unsolicited bid or
an activist path is open.

**Operating leverage (Phase 0): positive.** Revenue / operating income
($M): 2021 1,414 / 111 (7.8%); 2022 1,769 / 161 (9.1%); 2023 1,972 / 206
(10.4%); 2024 2,116 / 270 (12.8%); 2025 2,490 / 414 (16.6%); TTM Jun-2026
3,438 / 623 (18.1%). Revenue 2.4× while operating income went 5.6× —
margin has expanded every year, which is the bull case and the reversion
risk in one print (stockanalysis `/financials/income-statement/`).
