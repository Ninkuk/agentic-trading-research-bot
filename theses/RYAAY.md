## 2. Business

**Created:** the cheapest way to fly between roughly 220 European and North
African airports. FY26 average fare about €50 with 94% of seats filled; the
customer gets a new 737 on a punctual schedule at a price the legacy carriers
cannot match because Ryanair's break-even load factor is 80% and theirs is
far higher. The surplus is the fare gap: management's own figure is that
legacy unit costs run four to five times Ryanair's (call, management claim).
Ryanair also sells to airports — a base brings 2M seats a year of traffic
that an under-used regional airport cannot get elsewhere, which is why
Bratislava, Rabat and Tirana bid for it while Vienna, Berlin and Dublin lose
it.

**Captured:** three distinct mechanisms. (i) Scheduled fares, €10.56B in
FY26 — dynamically priced, sold 75–80% before the month starts, the lever
that swings the whole P&L (a 10% fare move is ~€1.0B of profit before tax).
(ii) Ancillaries, €4.99B in FY26 and €24 per passenger — priority boarding,
seats, bags, car hire, insurance; grows with traffic, guided +1–2% per
passenger, and the new EU bag-advertising rule is expected to be revenue
neutral (Ryanair DAC CFO). (iii) Cost arbitrage against everyone else:
owned aircraft bought at deep discounts to list (~60% basic credits on the
MAX-10, 20-F), no lease or finance cost, airport deals priced on volume
incentives, in-house airframe maintenance and, from 2029, in-house engine
shops with CFM. FY26 operating profit per passenger €11.39; the medium-term
target is €12–14 of net profit per passenger, mostly from yield (CEO).

**Protected:** a mechanism, not a label. The unit-cost gap is built from
things a rival cannot buy tomorrow — a single-type, owned, unencumbered fleet
of 620 aircraft bought at 2013/2014/2023 prices with 300 more on order at
credits no smaller customer receives; 95 bases whose airport contracts are
volume-priced; a 30,000-person operation that fills 94% of seats at a 96%
load in August; and a hedging book (80% of the coming year, rolled
opportunistically) that turns a fuel shock into a relative advantage. The
weakness in the mechanism is that everyone buys the same fuel and the same
Boeing: the moat is relative cost, and it protects share, not the absolute
level of profit in a year when fares are being stimulated. Growth is also
capped by Boeing's delivery pace — 4% traffic growth in FY27 is a supply
constraint, not a demand one.

**Control:** one share, one vote — for EU nationals only. Every ordinary
share or ADS held by or for a non-EU national (UK nationals included) has
been a "Restricted Share" with voting rights disapplied since 2021-01-01
under Article 41 of the Articles; the purchase restriction on non-EU buyers
was lifted 2025-03-07, but the vote was not restored (20-F Item 10). EU
nationals held ~30% of the shares at the last count (July 2026 call; next
count in the November H1 print). So a RYAAY holder in the US owns the
economics with no vote, and roughly 30% of the register — Capital Group
(14.6%) excluded — elects the board. Michael O'Leary holds 4.0% (42.2M
shares) and is roughly an eighth of the votes that count; his contract runs
to April 2032 with a new 10M-share option at €26.70/$65 that vests only on
PAT above €4.0B or 28 days above €42/$102, on top of 10M vested options at
€11.12 exercisable July 2027–February 2029 (6-K 2026-06-19; 20-F Item 6).
The structure forecloses a non-EU takeover outright (an EU operating licence
requires EU majority ownership and control, Regulation 1008/2008) and makes
a US activist path impossible; it does not foreclose an EU bidder, but at
€23B there is none. The 2026 AGM passed the remuneration policy with 61%
(6-K 2026-09-10).

**Operating leverage (Phase 0): positive over the cycle, negative in the
last two quarters — fare-driven in both directions.**
