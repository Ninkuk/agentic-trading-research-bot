## 2. Business

**Created:** A building owner needs land but does not need to *own* land. Land
does not depreciate, generates no operating income, and consumes roughly a
third of the capital in a development. Safehold buys the land and leases it
back for 30–99 years, so the developer converts an illiquid, zero-yield
equity stake in dirt into cash it can deploy into the building — typically at
a lower blended cost than a mortgage on the whole property, because the
ground lease sits senior to the leasehold mortgage and is therefore cheap
money. The customer preference being satisfied is capital efficiency, not
price: the tenant keeps the building's upside and rents the part of the asset
that has none.

**Captured:** Three distinct mechanisms, and they are not the same business.
(i) *Spread* — the portfolio's 3.8% annualized cash yield (5.5% on a GAAP
accrual basis) against a 3.9% cash / 4.4% effective cost of debt, levered
2.01×. This is the whole company and it is currently negative in cash.
(ii) *Escalation* — contractual rent bumps plus CPI lookbacks on 84% of cash
rent, against fixed-rate debt. This is where the spread is supposed to come
from, and it arrives over decades, not quarters. (iii) *Fees and adjacencies*
— $15.0M declining to $10.0M and then $7.5M per year for externally managing
Star Holdings, after which it resets to 2.0% of Star's gross book value as
Star liquidates; plus leasehold loans ($5M funded in Q2 at SOFR+463) and
joint-venture structuring. Item (iii) is shrinking on a schedule: "other
income" fell from $8.1M to $5.4M between H1 2025 and H1 2026, which is the
fee step-down almost exactly. Against ~$18–26M of annual recurring cash flow
to equity, losing the residual $7.5M fee is a third of the cash.

**Protected:** The honest answer is that the moat is a cost-of-capital moat
and it is currently running backwards. The ground lease structure itself is
excellent — 52% weighted-average ground-lease-to-value, 3.4× rent coverage,
senior to the leasehold mortgage — but nothing about it is proprietary.
Safehold's defensible claim was that it could fund 99-year land at a lower
cost than anyone else because it was the only scaled buyer. In Q2 2026 it
sold 49% of seven of its own ground leases to Brookfield for $348M. Brookfield
can originate directly. At 0.38× book, Safehold's equity cost of capital is
now plausibly the worst in its sector, which inverts the advantage: the
cheapest funder of ground leases is no longer the ground lease company. The
first-mover asset that remains is real but non-replicating — an existing book
of 3.9%-cash debt with an 18-year average life, which a new entrant cannot
buy at any price today.

**Control:** One class of common stock, no controlling holder. The material
structure is elsewhere: Star Holdings — a Maryland statutory trust spun out
of iStar in the March 2023 merger, holding the legacy non-ground-lease assets
— owns ~13.52M Safehold shares, 18.85% of the company, **all of it pledged as
collateral for a margin loan**, and Star is itself externally managed by a
Safehold subsidiary. A governance agreement restricts transfer and voting of
that block. Safehold's own Item 1A carries three separate risk factors on the
relationship (agreements it may be unable to collect on; the margin-loan
collateral; conflicts of interest) plus a fourth on "the concentration of our
voting power." Maryland-law and charter provisions are disclosed as
potentially inhibiting a change of control. Separately, the Caret program's
economics sit outside the common: Safehold holds ~84%, MSD Partners and other
family offices ~15%, employees ~1%, and the 10-K states plainly that Caret
distributions reduce distributions to common and that management's Caret
ownership creates conflicts. An unsolicited takeover is not foreclosed
outright, but a bidder must buy an 18.85% pledged block, inherit an external
management contract, and negotiate with a separate class of appreciation
holders.

The honest substitute is cash flow to equity built from the portfolio, paired
with market cap (net debt held at zero by the pairing rule — the flow is
already post-interest). Two independent routes:

- *Reported.* H1 2026 operating cash flow $26.035M less the $17.247M of
  non-recurring derivative proceeds = $8.788M recurring, ≈ **$17.6M**
  annualized.
- *Portfolio arithmetic.* Cash rent 3.8% × $7.1B GBV = $269.8M; less cash
  interest 3.9% × $4.98B = $194.2M; less cash G&A (TTM G&A ≈ $59.3M less SBC
  $13.149M) = $46.1M; less real estate expense ≈ $4.2M → **$25.3M**.

The two agree in order of magnitude, which is the point. SBC is deducted in
the second route ($13.149M TTM, 3.01% of revenue) and is already charged
against net income in the accrual scenario, so it is not double-counted.
Cash at 1.7% of market cap needs no excess-cash adjustment. Minority interests
rose from $31.6M to $193.1M in the half on the Brookfield JV; the
supplemental's $7.1B GBV and $4.98B debt are stated pro-rata, so the JV is
partly netted already — the residual error runs in the *generous* direction,
making the scenarios below flattering rather than harsh.

**Hurdle:** rf 5.01% (DGS10, 2026-09-18, `data/fred.db`) + beta 1.787 ×
ERP 4.14% (Damodaran implied ERP as of 2026-09-01, solved against his own
4.75% T-bond rate) = **12.41%**. Beta sits far outside the 0.8–1.2 stable
band and is not clamped: 2.01× leverage on ~92-year asset duration is a real
economic reason for it, and the anchors' clamp exists to floor artificially
*low* betas, not to cap justified high ones. The float check that triggers
that clamp does fire — float 54.64M of 70.82M shares out, 77.2%, under the
four-fifths line — but that is Star Holdings' restricted 18.85%, and it
pushes beta down if anything. Robustness: even at a band-clamped beta of 1.2
the hurdle is 9.98% and scenarios A–C still print negative spreads

Terminal growth is capped at the **10-year breakeven, 2.34% (T10YIE,
2026-09-21)**, used in every scenario.
