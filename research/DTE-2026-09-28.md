## 2. Business

**Created:** DTE Electric supplies power to about 2.3M customers in
southeastern Michigan. DTE Gas distributes gas across Michigan. The customer
gets reliable, regulated-price energy with no practical alternative supplier.
Residential electric bills run 17% below the national average and under 2% of
median household income (company claims, Q2 2026 call). Hyperscalers get
something scarcer: gigawatt-scale firm capacity on a 2027–2028 timeline.
Oracle and Google both chose DTE's territory for that.

**Captured:** three distinct mechanisms.
- **Regulated return on rate base.** Capital spent on generation, wires and
  pipe earns an allowed ROE (9.9% electric, 9.8% gas) on an equity layer of
  about 50%, set in periodic rate cases. The gas IRM surcharge recovers
  infrastructure capital between cases. Earnings grow with rate base, which
  grows with capex ($36.5B over five years). The utility is guided to about
  93% of earnings by 2030.
- **Special contracts with data centers.** Twenty-year supply agreements with
  minimum-billing provisions: DTE proposed 80% for Google, and the AG
  argued for 90% plus full-term termination payments. These fund new
  generation and absorb fixed costs; DTE claims $300M a year of benefit from
  Oracle and $1.7B over the life of the Google contract.
- **Non-utility.** DTE Vantage (RNG, customer energy solutions, behind-the-
  meter data-center projects outside Michigan): $45M in Q2 2026, and RNG tax
  credits are what move 2026 toward the high end. Energy Trading: $41M in
  Q2 2026, and its mark-to-market is the main gap between GAAP and operating
  EPS.

**Protected:** a legal franchise. A competitor cannot string a second set of
wires in Detroit, and Michigan's 10% retail-choice cap limits alternative
electric suppliers (market knowledge, not verified this run). The moat
protects the franchise, not the return. The return is set by the MPSC, and
the MPSC granted 42% and 46% of the last two asks. The excess return over
cost of capital is whatever the allowed ROE minus the cost of equity is,
roughly 1.3–3 points at today's hurdles (§4). Regulators are what compress
it when rates stay high.

**Control:** one share, one vote, no controlling holder. Insiders hold 0.44%
and institutions 82.1% (stocks.db); the float is 99.4% of shares
outstanding. Nothing forecloses a takeover structurally, but a utility
merger needs MPSC approval, which is the practical block.
