## 2. Business

**Created:** Arbor lends to multifamily (and now single-family-rental)
owners at two points in a property's life. First, short-term floating-rate
bridge loans to sponsors repositioning a property; second, the permanent
agency loan (Fannie Mae DUS / Freddie Mac) that takes the bridge out. The
borrower gets one counterparty from acquisition to permanent financing, with
speed and certainty of close. For 2021-vintage sponsors that "surplus" was
proceeds and leverage, which is exactly what has since gone bad.

**Captured:** four distinct mechanisms.
(a) **Net interest spread** on the $12.1B balance-sheet book: 6.95% all-in
yield against a 6.38% cost of debt, a 0.57% spot spread levered ~4×. It was
0.63% at 3/31.
(b) **Gain-on-sale** on agency originations: $1.05B in Q2 at a 1.33% margin,
down from 1.86% in Q1.
(c) **Servicing fees** on the $36.7B agency portfolio: 35bp weighted-average
fee, ~$128M gross a year, ~6-year remaining life.
(d) **MSR income** recognized at origination: $12M in Q2.
The bridge book also feeds the agency pipeline: bridge exits become agency
loans, which become servicing. That recycling loop is the franchise.
(Q2 2026 call; Q2 press release.)

**Protected:** a DUS licence is scarce (a short list of lenders) and the
servicing book is a contractual annuity. That is a real mechanism, but it
protects the fee stream, not the balance sheet. The bridge business has no
moat: Kaufman called it the "most competitive market" he has seen. And the
DUS licence carries first-loss sharing, so the moat comes with a credit
liability attached.

**Control:** one share, one vote; no controlling holder disclosed in this
run. Insiders hold 3.12% (`stocks.db`), institutions 64.8%. Kaufman
(Chairman/CEO) answered a go-private question with "all alternatives"
(Q2 call). Maryland REIT ownership limits are standard; nothing here
forecloses activism or a takeover. DEF 14A (2026-04-16) not read for the
exact chairman stake.
