## 1. Verdict and thesis

**PASS at $153.96.** kill-thesis: **FLAWED** — conditions=5 (2 probable,
3 plausible), refuted=1, unknown=0, pending=2, not_obtained=0.

**p(beat SPY, 63 td): 0.42** · kill-thesis: 0.45
**Disputed expectation:** the market is paying for roughly a decade of
double-digit free-cash-flow compounding from a franchisor whose own stated
algorithm delivers +12–15% *adjusted EPS* — a figure that is substantially
buyback arithmetic, not cash-flow growth. What revises this is either a price
near $110 or a demonstrated fee-margin step-up above the guided 100–150bps a
year.

This is a genuinely excellent business bought at a price that already contains
the excellence. IHG franchises and manages 7,109 hotels (1,049k rooms) and
takes a fee on $18.2bn of half-year system revenue while owning almost none of
the property: gross capital expenditure was $269m against $5.2bn of revenue in
FY2025, ROIC is 36.8%, and the fee business converted more than 100% of
adjusted earnings into adjusted free cash flow on a trailing basis. Operating
leverage is real and running the right way — H1 2026 fee revenue +7% on a fee
cost base +4%, lifting fee margin 120bps to 65.9%. None of that is in dispute.
The price is. On the honest owner-cash base — IHG's own adjusted free cash flow
less the capital items that measure excludes, less stock compensation — a
7%-a-year path for five years implies a 6.79% annual return against a 9.90%
cost of equity. Stretching every assumption to its generous limit (no haircuts
at all, 10% growth for a full decade) still lands at 9.64%, 26bp short. There
is no assumption set inside management's own stated algorithm that reaches the
hurdle at this price.

**Closest attack:** *the 9.90% hurdle is overstated, and the ceiling case
clears a fair one.* Substitute Damodaran's US headline implied ERP (4.14%,
1 September 2026) for the 4.83% operations-weighted figure and the hurdle
falls to 9.19%; the §4 ceiling case at 9.64% then clears it by 45bp. Cut beta
from 1.025 to a defensible franchisor 0.85 and the hurdle is 9.06% — cleared
by 58bp. This is the only attack that reaches the verdict, and it is the
reason the kill-thesis label is FLAWED-for-the-bull rather than something
stronger. It fails on two counts. First, the US-only ERP is the wrong input by
the reference's own rule for a company with 53.5% of segment revenue outside
the Americas. Second, and decisively, it requires the *maximum* growth case to
meet the *minimum* hurdle simultaneously — and conditions 2 and 4 are exactly
what say the maximum case cannot happen: the ceiling's 10%-for-a-decade path
needs fee margin near 78% by year 10, while condition 2 survives only at the
guided 100–150bps and adjusted-earnings growth has just decelerated from 11.0%
(FY2025) to ~8.6% (H1 2026). The runner-up attack — *the buyback converts a
7% aggregate path into ~11% per share* — is half right and fails as valuation
arithmetic: the reverse DCF already prices the whole cash flow stream against
the whole market cap, and a buyback is a use of that stream, not an addition
to it.

Load-bearing conditions of the bull case — what must be true for owning IHG at
$153.96 to beat SPY:

3. **The ~$950m-a-year buyback continues without pushing leverage out of the

4. **Adjusted EPS compounds at the stated +12–15% without interest expense

## 2. Business

**Created:** IHG sells hotel owners demand and sells travellers a predictable
room. To the owner — who supplies all the capital, buys the land, and takes the
property risk — IHG supplies a brand consumers recognise, a reservation system
that fills rooms at lower customer-acquisition cost than the owner could
manage alone, and a loyalty programme (IHG One Rewards) whose members book
direct rather than through an online travel agent. To the traveller it supplies
the thing hotels are actually bad at: knowing in advance what you will get. The
surplus is largest at the midscale end, where a Holiday Inn Express guest is
buying the absence of a bad surprise, and at the luxury end, where the
InterContinental and Six Senses flags are buying the owner a rate premium.

**Captured:** not "a franchise fee" — four distinct streams.
(i) *Royalty on room revenue* from franchised hotels, the core: H1 2026 fee
revenue of $971m on total gross system revenue of $18.2bn, an implied blended
take of roughly 5%.
(ii) *Management fees* on managed hotels — a base fee plus an incentive fee on
hotel profit, a geared claim on the same room revenue.
(iii) *Ancillary fee streams*, which are the margin story: sale of loyalty
points to consumers (a step-change to a $50m+ annual run-rate completed in
2025), and the US co-brand credit card agreements signed with Chase in
November 2024, which reached the expected $80m+ run-rate in 2025 and are
guided to $120m+ by 2028 — plus a further balance recognised inside System
Fund revenue. New co-brand agreements signed with Revolut/Visa in the UK (two
products launched 30 June 2026) and Sumitomo Mitsui Card/Visa in Japan
(launching 2027).
(iv) *Branded residences* — fees on residential units sold alongside luxury
hotels; 35 projects open or selling across 19 countries, with H1 2026 fees
from Six Senses Dubai Marina and InterContinental Halong Bay. Management
expects this to be more substantial from 2027.
Separately, the System Fund and reimbursables ($1,404m of H1 revenue, $2,644m
of the FY2025 IFRS total) run essentially at cost and are a pass-through, not
a profit pool — which is why every ratio computed on IHG's IFRS revenue line
understates the real margin by roughly half.

**Protected:** the mechanism is switching cost plus the two-sided nature of
the loyalty pool, not the brand name alone. A hotel owner who de-flags loses
the reservation stream on the day the sign comes down while still owing the
mortgage, and IHG collects key money ($179m in FY2025) precisely to lengthen
the contract that makes leaving expensive. The loyalty programme is the harder
half: a member with a points balance books IHG to protect the balance, and a
larger member base is what makes the flag worth paying for, so the two sides
reinforce. What this does **not** protect against is the take-rate itself.
Nothing stops an owner from converting to a rival flag at renewal — IHG wins a
great deal of its own growth exactly that way (a 14-hotel GCP Hospitality
conversion in Japan announced 24 August 2026; three Harilela hotels into
Vignette Collection on 17 August) — and nothing stops an online travel agent
or an AI travel assistant from re-intermediating the booking and compressing
what the flag is worth. IHG's own 20-F names both sides of this: "Guest
preferences for, or loyalty to, IHG-branded hotel experiences and channels"
and "Owner preferences for, or ability to invest in, our brands" are two of the
eleven refreshed 2026–2028 principal risks. The moat is real and it is a
contract-and-habit moat, not a structural one.

**Control:** one class of ordinary shares, one vote each, no controlling
holder. Largest disclosed interests at 12 February 2026: PineStone Asset
Management 8.07%, BlackRock 6.14%, Boron Investments B.V. 5.01%, FMR 5.01%,
Capital Group 4.90%. As a UK plc with a premium listing, IHG sits under the
Takeover Code — an unsolicited bid is structurally *easier* than for a US
incorporation, and there is no dual-class or founder block foreclosing an
activist. Insiders hold 0.26% of shares; float is 137.5M of ~146.6M shares
(93.8%). Nothing about the structure protects incumbency.

**Operating leverage (Phase 0): positive.** IFRS total revenue and operating
income, fiscal years plus TTM ($m):

| | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | TTM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| revenue | 2,318 | 3,892 | 4,624 | 4,923 | 5,189 | 5,329 |
| operating income | 503 | 699 | 1,025 | 1,046 | 1,213 | 1,261 |
| margin | 21.7% | 18.0% | 22.2% | 21.2% | 23.4% | 23.7% |

Revenue +130% from 2021 to TTM; operating income +151%. The 2021 base is
COVID-distorted, so the cleaner window is 2023→TTM: revenue +15.2%, operating
income +23.0%. The direction is unambiguously positive, and it is cleaner
still on the fee basis management runs the business on — H1 2026 fee revenue
+7% against a fee cost base +4%, fee margin 65.9% (+120bps).

## 3. Threads pulled

**The IFRS revenue line is not the business (resolved, and it explains a
screening artifact).** stockanalysis prints TTM revenue of $5,329m and a
forward P/S of 8.37 against a trailing P/S of 4.26 — a forward sales figure
roughly half the trailing one, which reads like a collapse. It is not. IFRS
total revenue consolidates the System Fund and reimbursables ($1,404m of H1
2026's $2,659m), which run at cost. Sell-side models sit on reportable-segment
revenue ($1,255m in H1) or fee revenue ($971m), roughly 47% and 36% of the
IFRS line. The same artifact drives stockanalysis's `revenue3y` estimate of
−17.18%. Nothing is wrong with the company; the two series are measuring
different things. Every margin figure in §2 is therefore quoted on the fee
basis where the comparison matters.

**Shareholder returns exceed free cash flow, and the gap is debt (the real
thread).** IHG returned $1,167m in FY2025 ($897m buyback + $270m dividends)
against adjusted free cash flow of $893m. Net cash flow before other net-debt
movements was **−$493m** for the year, and net debt rose $551m to $3,333m.
H1 2026 repeats it: $564m of returns, net debt +$330m to $3,663m. Management
is explicit and unembarrassed about this — the stated policy is to hold
leverage inside a 2.5–3.0x net-debt/adjusted-EBITDA band and return the
surplus, so growing EBITDA mechanically creates borrowing capacity that funds
the buyback. At 2.63x they have roughly $500m of headroom to the top of the
band on current EBITDA. This is a defensible policy, not a red flag, but it
means the headline "5.3% of market capitalisation returned" is **not** an
organic cash yield. The organic figure is adjusted FCF over market cap: 3.95%.
The distinction is the difference between a self-funding compounder and one
levering into a fixed target, and it is exactly what the Phase 4 base is built
to respect.

**The margin engine is decelerating, and IHG discloses the decomposition
itself.** Fee margin ran 61.2% (FY2024) → 64.8% (FY2025) → 65.9% (H1 2026).
The FY2025 jump of +3.6pts looks like a runaway operating-leverage story until
the 20-F breaks it out: "around 2.3%pts was driven by operational leverage,
including the benefits from our global efficiency programme, and a further
1.3%pts was due to incremental fees from the US co-brand credit card
agreements and from the sale of certain loyalty points". The half-year report
then says those two step-ups "achieved the anticipated incremental step-ups
... in 2024 and 2025, with growth in fees and fee margin therefore normalised
coming into 2026." H1 2026's +120bps is the first period *after* that
normalisation, and it is roughly half the FY2025 ex-step-up rate — landing in
the lower half of the guided 100–150bps band. The deceleration is real,
disclosed, and expected. It is also the arithmetic ceiling on the whole bull
case: from 65.9%, ten years at 125bps reaches 78.4%, which requires the fee
cost base to grow ~1.3% a year nominally for a decade — a real cost *decline*
in a business with 13,049 employees, and the very bottom of management's own
+1–3% guide sustained without interruption.

**The same disclosure splits adjusted EPS from adjusted earnings, and the gap
is the buyback.** FY2025: adjusted EPS +15.9% on adjusted earnings +11.0% —
4.9pts of the headline was share count. H1 2026: adjusted EPS +13% on ~8.6%
adjusted-earnings growth — 4.0pts share count. Management's "+12–15% adjusted
EPS" algorithm explicitly includes "the assumption of ongoing share buybacks",
so this is disclosure rather than a gotcha. But it means the cash-flow growth
that a reverse DCF can use is ~9%, not 12–15%, and it has decelerated ~240bp
in one half. §4's growth paths are set accordingly.

**Management is paid, in part, on a metric the buyback manufactures.** The
Annual Performance Plan weights 70% to operating profit from reportable
segments, 15% to openings and 15% to signings — all operational, none
gameable by capital allocation. The Long Term Incentive Plan is five equal 20%
legs: relative TSR, relative net system size growth, absolute cash flow
generation, **adjusted EPS**, and carbon/people. IHG's own 20-F, discussing
adjusted EPS, says it can be increased "through capital allocation, such as
the effect of increasing the measure by reducing the number of shares through
buybacks", and that it "has become an increasingly important measure as part
of IHG's ongoing return of surplus capital to shareholders, and is already a
defined performance measure within the LTIP." So one fifth of the long-term
award sits on a number a debt-funded repurchase mechanically lifts — the same
mechanism that is walking leverage from 2.4x to 2.63x. Stated fairly, the
incentive is mixed rather than captured: another 20% is absolute cash flow
generation, which buybacks do not flatter, 20% is relative TSR, which punishes
overpaying for your own shares, and the entire annual plan is operational. The
thesis does not require management to act against incentive. The problem is
the reverse — the bull case requires them to keep acting *with* it.

**"Asset-light" survives the commitments test, but the trailing capex line does
not tell you so.** stockanalysis prints TTM capex of $27m — 0.5% of revenue —
which would be a remarkable figure if it were the whole story. It is not: it
captures gross maintenance only. IHG's own gross capital expenditure for
FY2025 was $269m, comprising $179m of key money contract-acquisition costs,
$31m maintenance, $16m gross recyclable investments and $43m System Fund
capital investments; net capital expenditure was $185m. Key money is the real
recurring growth cost — it is what IHG pays owners to sign, it scales with the
signings that drive the growth path, and it belongs in the base FCF. IHG's own
adjusted-free-cash-flow measure already deducts key money and maintenance,
which is why Phase 4 builds on that measure rather than on the screen's `fcf`.
The verdict: the business is genuinely capital-light (net capex 3.6% of IFRS
revenue, 15% of segment revenue), but the honest number is $185–269m, not
$27m.

**Middle East exposure is bounded and management sized it.** EMEAA H1 RevPAR
growth decelerated from +5.6% in Q1 to +0.6% in Q2, which looks alarming until
the disclosure is read: the affected sub-region is 19% of EMEAA's system size
but **just 5% of IHG globally**, and it went −2% in Q1 then −19% in Q2. The
rest of EMEAA grew +7% and +4%. Americas — 66.5% of H1 segment operating
profit — accelerated, US RevPAR +3.4% in Q1 to +5.2% in Q2. The conflict is a
visible drag on a small denominator, not a thesis-level risk, and management
states it expects the impact to be fully offset by demand elsewhere. Thread
closes.

**The World Cup flatters the Americas comparison, and management said so
unprompted.** FIFA World Cup match locations added ~1.0pt to Americas Q2
RevPAR growth, some further benefit in July, and an estimated ~0.4pt to
Americas RevPAR growth for the full year 2026. That is a 2027 comparison
headwind of similar size, disclosed voluntarily. Credit where due — this is
the kind of thing managements usually leave for the analyst to find — but it
does mean the +4.8% Americas H1 print reads closer to +4.4% underlying, and the
Phase 4 growth path is set below the reported trajectory partly for this
reason.

**The growth path is explicitly a share-gain forecast, and that is the
aggressive part.** STR forecasts US industry net supply growth of +0.4% in
2026 and +0.8% in 2027 (cited in IHG's own H1 report), rising toward ~1% in
the three years after. IHG's net system growth is +5.0%. Sustaining roughly
six times industry supply growth for five years compounds the estate from
1,049k rooms to ~1,339k. It is not a "bigger than the market" forecast — it is
a conversion-share forecast, and the branded-share trend and IHG's own
conversion record support the direction — but it is the load-bearing
assumption behind any double-digit growth path, and it is why the Phase 4 base
case uses 7% FCF growth rather than the 9–10% the algorithm implies.

## 4. Valuation

**Inputs and pairing.** The flow is **levered** (equity, post-interest) and is
therefore paired with **market cap**; net debt is 0 by the pairing rule and
enterprise value is deliberately not used. Market cap $22,575,000,000
(146.63M shares implied by the statistics probe's exact market cap of
`22,674,893,363` at its own $154.64 stamp, restruck at the $153.96 close).

The base is built from IHG's own **adjusted free cash flow**, not from the
screen's `fcf`, because that measure already charges key money and maintenance
capex — the two items the screen's $27m capex line misses (see §3). TTM
adjusted FCF = FY2025 $893m + H1 2026 $360m − H1 2025 $302m = **$951m**. Two
haircuts follow:

- **Capital items adjusted FCF excludes:** gross recyclable investments and
  System Fund capital investments. TTM = (16+43) + (42+21) − (9+19) = **$94m**.
- **Stock compensation**, net of what is already charged. TTM SBC is $77m
  (`shareBasedComp`, 1.44% of revenue — low); employee-share-trust purchases
  already inside adjusted FCF are ~$15m TTM. Incremental deduction **$62m**.

Owner base FCF = 951 − 94 − 62 = **$795m**. Cross-check: TTM adjusted earnings
are 501.3¢ (FY2025) + 274.7¢ (H1 2026) − 242.5¢ (H1 2025) = 533.5¢, or ~$790m
on ~148M weighted shares — within 1% of the owner FCF, which independently
confirms management's ">100% conversion of adjusted earnings into adjusted free
cash flow" claim while stress-testing it. No minority-interest, pension or
litigation haircut applies (§3). Cash-heavy correction is not applicable: IHG
is net debt, not net cash.

**Hurdle: rf 4.95% + beta 1.025 × ERP 4.83% = 9.90%.** Risk-free is `DGS10`
from `data/fred.db`, 4.95% at 2026-09-10. Beta 1.025 from the statistics probe,
inside the 0.8–1.2 stable band with no clamp needed — insiders hold 0.26% and
float is 93.8% of shares out, so neither thin-float trigger fires. The ERP is
**operations-weighted**, not the US headline, because 53.5% of H1 segment
revenue is non-US: taking every leg from Damodaran's country table (one
vintage, last updated 5 January 2026) and reallocating the $192m Central
segment pro-rata gives weights of 54.9% Americas (US 4.46%), 36.8% EMEAA
(blended 5.30%) and 8.3% Greater China (5.14%) → **4.83%**. For reference his
headline monthly US implied ERP is 4.14% at 1 September 2026 against a T-bond
rate of 4.75%; that figure is *not* used here. Vintage mixing is stated: the
country table is a January 2026 vintage read against a September 2026
risk-free. The absolute companion check passes — rf + 4.5% = 9.45%, and 9.90%
sits just above it, as it should for a beta-1.0 name.

**Integrity checks.**

- *Reinvestment / growth-without-reinvestment warning — answered.* The tool
  flags it in every run: base FCF ($795m) exceeds trailing IFRS net income
  ($714m), so the terminal reinvestment rate solves negative (−0.113) and the
  2.36% terminal growth appears unpaid for. Two things resolve it. First, the
  earnings base genuinely is understated — IFRS net income carries $7m of H1
